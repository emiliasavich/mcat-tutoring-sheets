Glycolysis

The pathway that breaks a monosaccharide (usually glucose, but also fructose and galactose) down into pyruvate. One 6-carbon glucose is split into two 3-carbon pyruvates, capturing a little energy along the way. It runs in the cytoplasm and needs no oxygen.

glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP

Sticks a phosphate onto glucose. This costs one ATP up front — an investment that primes the molecule for the energy-releasing steps later.

Why phosphorylate first? The glucose transporter only moves unphosphorylated glucose, so adding the phosphate traps glucose inside the cell. It also keeps free intracellular glucose low, which keeps glucose flowing in.

The main control point of glycolysis — the rate-limiting, committed step. Whether sugar gets broken down is decided here.

The key regulatory decision. Because this step commits the cell to glycolysis, it's wired to the cell's energy status — heavily inhibited when energy is high and activated when energy is low. Full logic in the regulation section below.

Captures energy as the electron carrier NADH and builds a high-energy phosphate bond into the molecule.

That NADH has to be dealt with. It's either oxidized back to NAD⁺ by the mitochondrial electron transport chain (making ATP via oxidative phosphorylation, aerobic), or — when oxygen is scarce — regenerated by lactate dehydrogenase so glycolysis can keep running. NAD⁺ must be recycled or this step stalls.

First ATP-producing step. The high-energy phosphate built in the previous step is handed directly to ADP.

Substrate-level phosphorylation: ATP is made by transferring a phosphate straight from a high-energy substrate — no oxygen and no electron transport chain required. This is how glycolysis makes ATP even anaerobically.

The final step. Another substrate-level phosphorylation produces the second ATP of the payoff phase.

Feed-forward activation: pyruvate kinase is switched on by fructose-1,6-bisphosphate — the product of PFK-1. So the early commitment to glycolysis (made at PFK-1) reaches forward and pushes the pathway to finish.

How PFK-1 is controlled

Because PFK-1 sets the pace of the whole pathway, the cell regulates it directly with energy-status signals, and indirectly (in the liver) with hormones.

Activated by

  • AMP — signals the cell is low on energy and needs to make ATP
  • Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate — the liver's insulin signal (below)

Inhibited by

  • ATP — plenty of energy already, no need to break down more sugar
  • Citrate — a citric acid cycle intermediate; if it's building up, the cell is energy-rich

The liver's hormonal override — via PFK-2

The liver can keep glycolysis running even when ATP is high, so it can convert sugar into storage molecules. It does this through a second enzyme, PFK-2, which makes the activator fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP):

Insulin (fed state) → activates PFK-2 → ↑ F2,6BP → activates PFK-1 → glycolysis runs Glucagon (fasting) → inhibits PFK-2 → ↓ F2,6BP → PFK-1 stays off → glycolysis slows

Net effect: when insulin says "fed," the liver pushes glucose through glycolysis even past the ATP brake, then routes the products into glycogen, fatty acids, and other storage molecules.

The NAD⁺ problem & fermentation

GAPDH consumes NAD⁺. The cell only has a limited pool, so NAD⁺ must constantly be regenerated or glycolysis grinds to a halt. When oxygen is scarce, fermentation does that job.

When oxygen is poor, the electron transport chain can't accept electrons, so NADH piles up and NAD⁺ runs out. Pyruvate also accumulates. Fermentation solves both at once: it dumps the electrons from NADH onto pyruvate, regenerating NAD⁺ so GAPDH — and therefore glycolysis — can keep going.

In mammals — lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD⁺

The whole point is the NAD⁺ on the right — replenishing the oxidized coenzyme that GAPDH needs.

In yeast — alcoholic fermentation

pyruvate → ethanol + CO₂   (regenerating NAD⁺)

Different products, identical purpose: regenerate NAD⁺.

Intermediates worth knowing

A few molecules in the pathway have jobs beyond just being a step on the way to pyruvate.

  • Dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) — formed when fructose-1,6-bisphosphate is split. In liver and fat tissue, it's isomerized to glycerol-3-phosphate, which becomes glycerol — the backbone of triacylglycerols (fat synthesis).
  • 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate & PEP — the two high-energy intermediates that drive substrate-level phosphorylation, donating their phosphates to make the pathway's ATP.

Special case: red blood cells

Erythrocytes have no mitochondria, so they rely entirely on glycolysis — and they run a useful side-branch off it.

RBCs have an extra enzyme, bisphosphoglycerate mutase, that diverts 1,3-BPG into 2,3-BPG:

1,3-bisphosphoglycerate → 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG)

2,3-BPG lowers hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, which helps hemoglobin release O₂ to the tissues as blood passes through them.

The irreversible steps

Three steps in glycolysis are effectively one-way. They're the control points — and they're exactly the steps that gluconeogenesis (making new glucose) must route around using different enzymes.
