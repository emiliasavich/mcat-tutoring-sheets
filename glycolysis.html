Zooming in on glycolysis

Glycolysis takes in a six-carbon monosaccharide (typically glucose) and turns it into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules. This reaction occurs in the cytosol and does not need oxygen. Each pyruvate is then converted to acetyl‑CoA, the molecule the TCA cycle needs to begin, with the help of the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase.

Hexokinase / Glucokinase
glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP

The enzymes move a phosphate group from ATP onto glucose. Each enzyme has its own characteristics.

Hexokinase

  • Found in most tissues.
  • Has a high affinity for glucose (a low Km), so it binds to and phosphorylates glucose rapidly even when glucose concentrations are low. This lets the tissues use glucose at low concentrations.
  • Inhibited by its own product, glucose-6-phosphate (G6P): as G6P builds up, hexokinase slows down, so a cell only uses as much glucose as it needs to meet its energy demand.

Glucokinase

  • Found in the liver and pancreatic β-cells.
  • Has a low affinity for glucose (a high Km), so it only becomes active when glucose concentrations are high and there is excess to be stored. This lets the liver take up and process the excess only when there is glucose to spare, rather than competing with other tissues when glucose is limited.
  • Induced by insulin: high insulin increases expression of glucokinase, which makes the liver better able to take up and process glucose.

Km is inversely related to affinity (low Km = high affinity). What is Km? ↓

Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1)

The enzyme moves a phosphate from another ATP to the Spends a second ATP to add another phosphate — and this is the pathway's big decision point. PFK-1 is the rate-limiting, irreversible step: once it fires, the molecule is committed to being broken all the way down to pyruvate. Because this step commits the cell's resources, it is tightly regulated — in two separate ways.

1 · The cell's energy level (direct control)

PFK-1 senses how much energy the cell already has and adjusts its rate to match — speeding up when energy is low, slowing down when energy is high:

Activated by ▲
AMP — builds up as ATP gets used, signalling the cell is low on energy and needs glycolysis to run.
Inhibited by ▼
ATP — energy is already plentiful, so there's no need to make more · citrate — a TCA-cycle intermediate; if it's piling up, the cell is already energy-rich.

2 · Hormones, in the liver (indirect control)

The liver can override those energy signals so it can manage glucose for the whole body. Insulin and glucagon don't act on PFK-1 directly — instead they change the level of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F-2,6-BP), a dedicated activator of PFK-1:

  • Insulin (high blood glucose, after a meal) raises F-2,6-BP, switching PFK-1 on — so the liver pushes glucose through glycolysis and stores the surplus as glycogen and fat. Makes sense: glucose is abundant, so use and store it.
  • Glucagon (low blood glucose, fasting) lowers F-2,6-BP, switching PFK-1 off — so the liver stops burning glucose, sparing it for tissues that depend on it like the brain and red blood cells. Makes sense: glucose is scarce, so save it.

6 GAPDH make NADH details
Enzyme
Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH)
G3P + Pᵢ + NAD⁺ → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + NADH

This is where glycolysis first banks energy. The enzyme pulls energy out of the sugar and loads it onto NAD⁺, converting it into NADH. Think of NAD⁺ as a reusable shuttle: NAD⁺ is the empty form, and once it's loaded with energy from the sugar it becomes NADH, the loaded form — which can be cashed in for a lot of ATP later. At the same time, the enzyme builds a high-energy phosphate bond into the molecule.

The catch: the cell only has a small pool of NAD⁺, so it must constantly be regenerated, or this step — and all of glycolysis — grinds to a halt. There are two ways out:

Aerobic
The electron transport chain turns NADH back into NAD⁺ — and that drives oxidative phosphorylation to make lots of ATP.
Anaerobic
When oxygen is scarce, fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ — making lactate (in mammals, via LDH) or ethanol + CO₂ (in yeast).

1,3-bisphosphoglycerate

One of the pathway's two high-energy intermediates. The phosphate bond it carries is energetic enough to be handed straight to ADP — that's what powers the substrate-level phosphorylation at the next step (phosphoglycerate kinase) to make ATP.

RBC branch: red blood cells can divert 1,3-BPG into 2,3-BPG, which lowers hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen so it releases O₂ to the tissues.

The finish line. A final phosphate is transferred from PEP straight to ADP — a second substrate-level phosphorylation — producing the payoff phase's second ATP and leaving pyruvate, the end product of glycolysis.

Why the irreversible steps matter

Three steps in the map are effectively one-way — step 1 (hexokinase / glucokinase), step 3 (PFK-1), and step 10 (pyruvate kinase), each tagged irreversible above. That one-way character is exactly what makes them valuable:

  • They're the control points. Because these reactions are far from equilibrium, dialing their enzymes up or down actually changes how fast the whole pathway runs — so the cell concentrates its regulation here, above all at PFK-1, the rate-limiting step.
  • Gluconeogenesis must route around them. Making new glucose can't simply run glycolysis backward through these three — each one needs its own dedicated bypass enzyme to get around it.

Hexokinase vs. Glucokinase

Both catalyze the same first step, but they're tuned for different jobs — this contrast is a classic exam favorite. The difference comes down to Km, so it's worth pinning down what that means first.

What Km means

Km — the Michaelis–Menten constant — is the substrate concentration at which an enzyme runs at half its maximum velocity (½ Vmax). At that concentration, on average half the active sites are occupied by substrate.

Km = [substrate] at which velocity = ½ Vmax

It's used as a readout of affinity — how tightly the enzyme grips its substrate — and the relationship is inverse: an enzyme that binds tightly already hits half-speed while substrate is still scarce, so it has a low Km.

Low Km → high affinityReaches half-speed at low substrate; works even when substrate is scarce.
High Km → low affinityNeeds a lot of substrate to get going; only ramps up when substrate is plentiful.

How PFK-1 is controlled

Because PFK-1 sets the pace of the whole pathway, the cell regulates it directly with energy-status signals, and indirectly (in the liver) with hormones.

Activated by

  • AMP — signals the cell is low on energy and needs to make ATP
  • Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate — the liver's insulin signal (below)

Inhibited by

  • ATP — plenty of energy already, no need to break down more sugar
  • Citrate — a citric acid cycle intermediate; if it's building up, the cell is energy-rich

The liver's hormonal override — via PFK-2

The liver can keep glycolysis running even when ATP is high, so it can convert sugar into storage molecules. It does this through a second enzyme, PFK-2, which makes the activator fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP):

Insulin (fed state) → activates PFK-2 → ↑ F2,6BP → activates PFK-1 → glycolysis runs Glucagon (fasting) → inhibits PFK-2 → ↓ F2,6BP → PFK-1 stays off → glycolysis slows

Net effect: when insulin says "fed," the liver pushes glucose through glycolysis even past the ATP brake, then routes the products into glycogen, fatty acids, and other storage molecules.

Regenerating NAD⁺

In step 6, GAPDH takes one NAD⁺ and turns it into NADH. However, cells have a limited amount of NAD⁺, so for glycolysis to keep running, NADH must be turned back into NAD⁺ as quickly GAPDH uses it. If NAD⁺ becomes scarce, step 6 and, consequently, all of glycolysis slows. To avoid this, cells have two methods to regenerate NAD⁺:

With oxygen — the electron transport chain

When oxygen is available, NADH travels to the electron transport chain in the mitochondria, where it is turned back into NAD⁺. This both regenerates NAD⁺ (so glycolysis continues) and powers ATP synthesis.

Without oxygen — lactate fermentation

With little or no oxygen, the electron transport chain slows or stops. This creates two problems:

  • NAD⁺ becomes scarce. With the electron transport chain slowed, less NADH is converted back to NAD⁺.
  • Pyruvate builds up. Normally pyruvate (the end product of glycolysis) would be turned to acetyl-CoA and enter the TCA cycle. However, the TCA cycle also depends on NAD⁺, so when NAD⁺ is scarce, the cycle slows down and becomes less able to use pyruvate.

Fermentation solves both problems. The built-up pyruvate is used to turn NADH back into NAD⁺, and in mammals, the reaction is mediated by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH).

pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD⁺
